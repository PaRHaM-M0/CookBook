Homemade Mozzarella

Fresh Mozzarella, Stovetop Method

This mild, soft mozzarella is made with pasteurized cow’s milk, citric acid and rennet, then stretched in hot water. It is a fresh cheese, ready to eat without aging.

Yield
About 1 pound (450 g)

Cultured Pizza-Style Mozzarella

This cow’s-milk mozzarella uses a thermophilic starter and warm curd ripening before stretching, shaping and brining. It is intended for pizza and baked dishes. The moisture percentage is unmeasured; the cheese has not been shown to meet the low-moisture mozzarella standard.

Before you start

  • Rennet strengths differ. This quantity is for single-strength liquid rennet; check the manufacturer’s directions for a different product.
  • Choose pasteurized whole milk not labeled ultra-pasteurized, ultrapasteurized or UHT. Brands behave differently and a successful curd is not guaranteed.
  • Wash hands and clean and sanitize equipment and surfaces touching milk or cheese; follow sanitizer directions and keep residue out of ingredients. Chill the bath water and measure the salt before starting; refrigerate the rennet as directed.
  • Equipment: stainless-steel pot at least 6 quarts (5.7 L), second pot or kettle, accurate thermometer, long knife, slotted and large spoons, colander, large heat-safe bowls, clean small cup for whey, food-safe heat-resistant waterproof gloves or two sturdy spoons, clean bath bowl and covered storage container.
  • Estimates are rounded. Active time covers preparation, heating, cutting, cooking, draining, bath preparation, stretching and shaping. Stretching water heats alongside milk and reserved whey cools while you work; neither is counted twice. Heating, setting and stretching depend on milk and equipment.

Burn warning: hot water and curd can scald. Use food-safe heat-resistant waterproof gloves or sturdy spoons; thin disposable gloves alone are not sufficient.

Phase 1

Acidify and Heat the Milk

  1. Dissolve the citric acid.

    Stir the citric acid into the 1 cup (240 mL) cool water, then pour into the large pot. Dissolving the crystals disperses the acid evenly; concentrated pockets may cause uneven curdling.

  2. Add the cold milk.

    Pour milk into the solution and stir thoroughly, leaving no separate layer. Even acidity helps the curd stretch later. Allow 5–10 minutes total for these first two steps.

  3. Heat the milk and stretching water.

    Slowly heat the milk to 90°F (32°C), stirring gently and checking with a thermometer, about 15–30 minutes. Avoid overshooting; a little curdling near the target can be normal. At the same time heat a separate pot of potable water to 175°F (79°C) for stretching.

Phase 2

Set, Cut and Cook the Curd

  1. Add the rennet.

    Dilute the rennet in ¼ cup (60 mL) cool non-chlorinated water immediately before use. At 90°F (32°C), remove the milk from heat, add the diluted rennet and stir gently top to bottom for about 30 seconds. Stop the milk moving, cover and leave undisturbed. Even distribution helps setting; continued stirring breaks the forming gel.

  2. Check the set.

    After 5 minutes, check for a custard-like curd that separates from the whey when gently cut with a clean knife. If very soft or the whey is milky, cover and wait up to about 30 additional minutes. Cutting too early can produce fines and poor yield.

  3. Cut the curd.

    With a long knife cut a roughly 1-inch (2.5-cm) checkerboard to the bottom of the pot, then proceed directly to cooking. Recognizable blocks should be surrounded by whey; small or rough cuts release more moisture and can make cheese drier.

  4. Cook and stir.

    Return to low heat and slowly warm the curds to 110°F (43°C), stirring gently. Allow about 12–20 minutes for cutting and cooking combined. The curds become more distinct and release whey; vigorous stirring breaks them into small particles.

  5. Finish firming.

    Remove from heat and stir slowly for 2–5 minutes. Curds should feel slightly firmer while holding together; longer stirring releases more moisture and can make firmer cheese.

Phase 3

Drain and Prepare the Cooling Bath

  1. Drain and salt.

    Scoop curds into a colander over a bowl. Reserve 2 tablespoons (30 mL) clear whey in a clean cup and refrigerate to cool; return the remaining collected whey to the pot and rest the colander over it to keep the curds warm. Sprinkle ½ teaspoon non-iodized cheese salt over the curds. They should form a soft mass as excess whey runs off; avoid heavy pressing or working for a moist cheese.

  2. Prepare the bath.

    Dissolve 2 tablespoons non-iodized cheese salt in 1 quart (950 mL) very cold water. Keep cold while the reserved whey cools during stretching; add it before chilling. Allow 5–10 minutes combined for draining and bath preparation.

Phase 4

Stretch and Shape

  1. Heat the curd.

    Transfer some 175°F (79°C) stretching water to a heat-safe bowl. Cut or break the curd into 1–2-inch (2.5–5-cm) pieces; place in water and press and fold with two spoons or protected hands until softened and beginning to meld. A cool, firm center resists stretching.

  2. Stretch.

    Lift the softening curd, stretch gently and fold it over itself several times. It should become cohesive, smooth, shiny and stretch like taffy. If it tears as it cools, briefly return it to hot water; stop when smooth to avoid tough, dry cheese.

  3. Shape.

    Tuck the warm cheese under itself to form a ball and pinch the seam underneath. A smooth surface and joined seam show it holds together; excessive squeezing expels moisture. Allow about 10–15 minutes combined for heating, stretching and shaping.

Phase 5

Cool

  1. Chill in the cold bath.

    Add the reserved 2 tablespoons (30 mL) cooled clear whey to the very cold salt bath. Chill the cheese for about 10–20 minutes until cool and firm enough to hold its shape. This is a short cooling bath, not storage brine; drain, transfer promptly to a clean covered container and refrigerate.

Troubleshooting

  • Milk fails to form a firm gel: allow the extra time in step 5. If still liquid or weak, check milk heat treatment and rennet freshness before another batch; do not add more rennet to force it.
  • Curd breaks into tiny pieces: it may have been cut too early or stirred too hard. Handle the remaining curd gently; texture and yield can still suffer.
  • Curd will not stretch: check water is near 175°F (79°C) and the curd heated through. If it still breaks, milk or acidity may be unsuitable; do not add arbitrary acid.
  • Rubbery or dry cheese: too much whey may have been lost. Next time handle gently and stop stretching as soon as smooth.
  • Very soft cheese: chill thoroughly before slicing. Fresh mozzarella is naturally moist and does not behave like firm pizza cheese.
  • Mold, slime, swelling or off-odor: discard without tasting. Normal smell and appearance do not prove safety.

Use and storage

Enjoy fresh with tomatoes, on toast or on pizza. Keep covered at 40°F (4°C) or below. As a conservative editorial recommendation use within 3 days; this is not a tested or validated shelf life for this homemade batch.

Editorial notes

The primary source lists 1 teaspoon cheese salt in ingredients but ¼–½ teaspoon in draining directions. The ½ teaspoon for curds is an editorial choice, not a tested improvement. The 10–20-minute cooling time, elapsed-time estimates and three-day storage recommendation are editorial estimates. This recipe has not been personally kitchen-tested.

Alternatives to C201 Starter

For this recipe’s quantity of milk, choose only one culture option from the ingredient table; do not add the cultures together. This section applies to the cultured mozzarella, not the fresh citric-acid version.

  • More accessible option: plain yogurt with live cultures. The base recipe recommends 3.5–5 oz (about 99–142 g) of prepared Bulgarian Y1 yogurt instead of C201 for this quantity of milk. For a store-bought option, choose plain yogurt without sugar, flavoring, fruit or thickeners. The label should confirm live cultures, preferably Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus bulgaricus; it must not have been heat-treated after culturing to deactivate them. Do not substitute drinking yogurt, kefir or flavored yogurt.
  • The source’s amount and timing are for yogurt prepared with Y1, not every commercial yogurt. Using the same amount of plain store-bought live-culture yogurt is an experimental, untested adaptation of this recipe: strains and activity vary by brand, so acidification time, stretching and results are not guaranteed. At 98°F (37°C), smooth the yogurt with a little warmed milk and stir it into the full batch; there is no dry powder to rehydrate. For prepared Y1, ripen the milk at that temperature for 30 minutes as the source directs. For store-bought yogurt, 30 minutes may be used as an experimental starting point, not a validated time; then follow the subsequent steps and stretch tests.
  • Another powdered option: Thermo B. For this recipe’s quantity of milk the recipe source specifies ⅛ teaspoon in place of C201; sprinkle and rehydrate for 2 minutes as with C201, stir, then ripen the milk for 60 minutes at 98°F (37°C). The product page suggests ¼ teaspoon for 1–2 gallons, which differs from this recipe-specific amount. Check product strength and manufacturer directions; do not assume packets are interchangeable or combine the doses.

For any substitute, control temperature and sanitation and follow the stretch tests. If repeated tests show no progress, do not hold the curd warm indefinitely or overnight; discard it. A stretch test or pH reading alone does not prove safety, and the three-day storage suggestion remains unvalidated. These substitutions have not been personally kitchen-tested in this recipe.

Before you start

  • Choose pasteurized cow’s milk not labeled UHT, ultra-pasteurized or ultrapasteurized. Homogenized milk can be used, but brands vary in curd formation. The 1% low-fat milk is an editorial choice.
  • Keep C201 culture in the freezer and rennet refrigerated as directed. One C201 packet is intended for up to 2 gallons. The 3.5 mL rennet dose is the editorial midpoint of the source’s 3–4 mL range. Do not substitute different-strength rennet or calcium chloride measure for measure.
  • Wash hands and clean and sanitize equipment, cloth and surfaces contacting milk or cheese. Follow sanitizer directions and keep residue out of ingredients.
  • Chill the brine before the cheesemaking session, check the thermometer, prepare the warm-curd setup and make room in a refrigerator at 40°F (4°C) or below. Complete warm incubation in one session; do not leave curds warming overnight or indefinitely.
  • The total includes about 8 hours of advance brine chilling plus 8½–11½ hours for other work and waiting, including mixing. The 8 hours is a planning allowance, not a guarantee: check the brine has reached 36–40°F (2–4°C). Active time includes incubation and brining checks and turns; these are not counted twice. Test-water heating overlaps incubation. The optional overnight rest afterward is excluded.
  • Calcium-chloride conflict: the pizza recipe calls for it in pasteurized milk and this version follows that dose; the same supplier’s product page warns against it in mozzarella because it may prevent stretching. This conflict is unresolved; do not add extra to rescue a batch.
  • Brining at 36–40°F (2–4°C) for 2–2½ hours is a refrigerated adaptation, not validated for this exact recipe; salt uptake may differ from the source’s warmer schedule. Brining seasons the cheese, but is not proof of preservation or safety.
  • Equipment: stainless-steel pot at least 12 quarts (11 L) with lid; larger pot or clean sink for warm-water bath; accurate and refrigerator thermometers; long curd knife; slotted ladle; large colander, clean butter muslin and whey bowl; clean cooler with warm-water bottles or setup holding colander above warm water; small heat-safe cup and spoon for test; optional calibrated cheese pH meter or suitable narrow-range food-grade strips; second pot and heat-safe bowl; food-safe heat-resistant waterproof gloves and two sturdy spoons; ice-water bowl; covered nonreactive brine vessel; clean draining rack and covered refrigerator container; measuring spoons, cup and a small mL syringe for rennet.

Burn warning: hot water and curd can scald. Use food-safe heat-resistant waterproof gloves or sturdy spoons; thin disposable gloves alone are not sufficient.

Phase 1

Prepare the Brine in Advance

  1. Mix and chill.

    In a clean nonreactive vessel stir together 1 gallon (3.785 L) potable water, 2¼ pounds (1.02 kg) cheese salt, 1 tablespoon (15 mL) calcium chloride solution and 1 teaspoon (5 mL) white vinegar. Cover and refrigerate; plan 8 hours and check the brine reaches 36–40°F (2–4°C) before use.

  2. Confirm saturation.

    At 36–40°F (2–4°C), stir thoroughly and add extra salt in small amounts until some remains undissolved after mixing and settling. Remaining crystals are a practical saturation cue at use temperature; the starting weight alone does not guarantee saturation. Keep covered and refrigerated.

Phase 2

Warm and Culture the Milk

  1. Heat the milk.

    Pour both measured amounts of milk into the large pot and warm slowly to 98°F (37°C) using a warm-water bath or low direct heat, about 20–30 minutes. Stir regularly if heating directly and check in several places for even heating without scorching.

  2. Add calcium chloride.

    While heating, dilute ½ teaspoon (2.5 mL) calcium chloride in ¼ cup (60 mL) cool non-chlorinated water and stir thoroughly into the milk. The pizza recipe includes it to aid setting, subject to the conflict above.

  3. Add the chosen culture.

    At 98°F (37°C), for C201 or Thermo B sprinkle the measured powder evenly over the milk, leave 2 minutes, then stir top to bottom for 2 minutes until dispersed. For yogurt, smooth it with a little warm milk and stir into the batch; powder rehydration is not needed. See the culture-alternatives section for details and limitations.

  4. Ripen the milk.

    Cover and check the temperature, holding at 98°F (37°C). With C201 or Thermo B, hold for 60 minutes; with prepared Y1 yogurt, 30 minutes as the source directs. With store-bought live-culture yogurt, 30 minutes is only an experimental starting point, not a validated time or result. The milk may look much the same as acidity develops; continue and follow the later curd stretch tests.

Phase 3

Set, Cut and Drain the Curd

  1. Add the rennet.

    Immediately before use dilute 3.5 mL single-strength rennet in ¼ cup (60 mL) cool non-chlorinated water. Add to ripened milk, stir slowly bottom to top for 1 minute, stop the milk moving and cover for an undisturbed gel.

  2. Check the set.

    Leave undisturbed 24 minutes, then insert a clean knife at an angle and lift gently. Proceed when it separates as a firm cohesive gel. If weak, wait up to about 12 minutes longer; cutting a fragile set makes fines.

  3. Make the first cut.

    Cut the firm curd evenly to approximately 1-inch (2.5-cm) pieces reaching the bottom, then rest 5 minutes. Recognizable pieces remain surrounded by whey; resting firms their cut surfaces.

  4. Cut smaller and rest.

    Gently cut to approximately ½–⅝ inch (1.3–1.6 cm) and rest 10 minutes. If temperature dropped, bring it slowly back to 98°F (37°C) during the rest. Smaller pieces release whey; gentle handling limits crumbling.

  5. Stir and settle.

    Stir slowly for about 10 minutes without crushing, then stop and let settle. Pieces become more distinct as they release whey; prolonged or vigorous stirring can make cheese drier.

  6. Drain.

    Ladle whey off to the surface of the settled curds, then transfer curds to the muslin-lined colander over a bowl. They collect into a soft mass while whey drains; consolidation limits further moisture loss during acid development.

Phase 4

Ripen and Test the Curd

  1. Incubate.

    Place the covered colander in the warm setup and maintain curd at 90–95°F (32–35°C) for about 2–2½ hours, turning every 20–30 minutes. It should knit together while draining and the culture develops acidity.

  2. Perform the stretch test.

    Shortly before testing, heat potable water and measure immediately before use: 160–165°F (71–74°C). Around 2 hours, immerse a thin strip in a cup of hot water until heated through; lift and gently pull with a spoon or protected hand. A passing strip stretches under its own weight into a long strand; a quickly breaking strip is not ready.

  3. Retest if necessary.

    If it breaks, check test-water temperature, return the main curd to 90–95°F (32–35°C) and test a fresh strip after 20–30 minutes. Reheat and measure water immediately before every test; several early tests are better than missing the window because over-ripened curd can be sour and lack structure. A calibrated meter or suitable strip may guide: the source gives about pH 5.3. Neither pH nor stretching alone proves safety. If repeated tests show no progress, stop rather than holding warm indefinitely or overnight. Do not add unmeasured acid, rennet or calcium chloride.

Phase 5

Stretch and Shape

  1. Heat curd strips.

    Shortly before stretching, heat or reheat potable water and measure immediately before use: 160–165°F (71–74°C). Cut ripe curd into thin strips, pile loosely in a heat-safe bowl and pour water around the edge until covered. Keep separate until several pieces stretch into long strands without breaking.

  2. Stretch and fold.

    Gather softened strips, lift so their weight stretches them, then fold several times. Stop when joined, elastic, smooth and glossy; overworking makes cheese tough and dry. If curd stiffens and water cools, safely pour off some and replenish with water measured at 160–165°F (71–74°C), then briefly reheat the curd.

  3. Shape.

    Fold the warm mass into a compact ball or short cylinder, turning the surface inward and pinching the seam underneath. A smooth exterior and closed seam show it holds; if cool and resistant briefly return to hot water rather than pulling hard. Allow 20–30 minutes combined for heating, stretching and shaping.

Phase 6

Chill, Brine and Dry

  1. Chill.

    Transfer to a clean ice-water bath for about 30 minutes until cold and firm enough to hold its shape. The source calls for ice-water chilling without a specified duration.

  2. Brine under refrigeration.

    Place in covered saturated brine at 36–40°F (2–4°C) for 2–2½ hours, checking refrigerator temperature. Sprinkle a little cheese salt on exposed cheese; turn halfway through and salt the newly exposed surface.

  3. Surface-dry cold.

    Remove with clean utensils to a clean rack in a covered refrigerator container for about 1–2 hours, turning once. Proceed when no longer visibly wet; this does not establish internal moisture percentage.

  4. Wrap and refrigerate.

    Wrap or place in a clean covered container at 40°F (4°C) or below. Once thoroughly cold and firm enough to handle, it is ready; chilling does not establish low-moisture classification. Discard used brine rather than storing the cheese in it.

Troubleshooting

  • Weak or absent curd: allow the additional time in step 8. If still not cohesive the batch is unlikely to make mozzarella; do not add rennet. Check milk treatment, thermometer and rennet next time.
  • Failed stretch test: measure test water immediately before use and heat a fresh strip through. Briefly continue controlled incubation and retest; if repeated tests show no progress stop and discard rather than holding warm indefinitely.
  • Sour or crumbly curd may have passed the stretching window; extra stretching or additives will not reliably restore it. Discard if handling or incubation was uncontrolled or spoilage suspected; do not taste a questionable batch.
  • Too wet to grate: chill thoroughly and try dicing for baked dishes. Extra surface-drying does not reliably correct internal moisture.
  • Tough, dry, oily or salty defects are hard to reverse. If safely handled use small amounts in a baked dish; next time handle gently, stop stretching when smooth and record brining time.
  • Brine or temperature problems: the stated schedule and salt uptake may not apply. A deviation alone does not determine safety; if handling history is uncertain or contamination suspected, discard rather than attempting to preserve with more brining or aging.
  • Swelling, slime, mold or off-odors: discard the whole batch without tasting. Normal appearance, smell or flavor does not prove safety.

Use and storage

Once thoroughly cold and firm, grate for pizza or dice for baked pasta. An overnight refrigerator rest may help handling, but commercial grateability and melting are not guaranteed; this unaged version will not necessarily match the source’s aged cheese. Keep covered at 40°F (4°C) or below. As a conservative editorial recommendation use within 3 days or freeze promptly; this is not a validated safety limit. Thaw in the refrigerator and use in a cooked dish.

Editorial notes

The source recommends 10–14 days aging in one place and 2–4 weeks at 36–40°F elsewhere; these quality recommendations are not a validated safety shelf life. This version has no extended aging. The 1% milk, rennet midpoint, advance brine chilling, refrigerated brining, 30-minute ice bath, cold surface-drying and three-day recommendation are editorial choices. The pizza source specifies 50–52°F for 2–2½ hours. This assembled recipe has not been personally kitchen-tested; moisture, salt uptake and shelf life have not been measured or validated.
